Before You Call Someone Careless

We often explain our own behavior with context and other people's with character. That shortcut can make us miss the pressures and conditions that shaped what happened.

In Brief

  • You had reasons. They had flaws. That is the double standard, and it can be hard to notice while we are living it.
  • Context is not an excuse. It is part of the cause. Miss it and you may punish where you should redesign.
  • The work is to catch the sentence before it hardens.

You had reasons. They had flaws.

That is how the mistake can feel from the inside. One easy mistake is to believe that behavior explains itself. Someone is sharp in a meeting, and we decide they are arrogant. Someone misses a deadline, and we decide they are careless. Someone cuts us off in traffic, and we decide they are reckless.

We take a moment and turn it into a judgment about a person. The move is so quick that it does not feel like interpretation. It feels like perception. But behavior does not happen in isolation. It happens under pressure and inside a situation. Sometimes we are seeing a person collide with context we do not understand.

Two moral systems

Psychologists gave this tendency a name. In 1977, Lee Ross described the fundamental attribution error: our tendency to explain other people's behavior through their character while giving too little weight to their situation.

A few years earlier, Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett described a related pattern called the actor-observer asymmetry. We explain our own actions through circumstance and other people's actions through character. When someone else behaves badly, we may call them lazy, difficult, or selfish. When we behave badly, we may say we were exhausted, under pressure, or working with poor information. The double standard is obvious on paper and nearly invisible while we live it. We do not feel as if we are using two moral systems. We feel as if we are being accurate. Other people start to look simpler than they are, while we give ourselves the more complete story.

I catch myself doing this more than I would like. As a founder, it is easy to call someone careless when I did not manage them well, or to call a team slow when I had not made the priorities clear. I have been on the other side too. People inside my company have read my shifting priorities as impulsiveness, as evidence that I cannot hold a belief. From inside my head, the shifts are part of one connected line of thought. They see character. I see circumstance. We can both make the mistake this essay describes. The character story is always available, and it tends to flatter the person telling it.

One reason the bias can persist is that explanations about character feel clean. If someone disappoints us, it is simpler to decide they are unreliable than to hold a more complicated picture. Character stories give us a quick sense of closure. That speed can be useful, but it can also mislead us. Human behavior rarely has only one cause. Someone may be careless, and also exhausted, poorly managed, or afraid to raise a concern. The behavior may be real while our explanation for it is wrong. When the explanation hardens too quickly, we stop investigating. Premature certainty is the danger.

Context deepens the diagnosis

This is where people can get uncomfortable. They hear an emphasis on situation and assume it means going soft. It does not. Context does not erase responsibility. It can make the diagnosis more complete.

If a person in authority humiliates someone publicly, we still need to address what they did. It may also matter whether the environment normalized aggression and rewarded fear-based speed. If a frontline employee seems disengaged, their behavior matters. So might the conditions of a punishing role or incentives that work against one another. A person remains responsible for their actions, but a fuller explanation can help us choose a useful response.

Shallow diagnosis is one source of recurring failure in families, teams, and institutions. A company sees poor execution and calls someone weak instead of asking whether the system is creating confusion. We can find it easier to condemn a person than to understand the conditions around them.

This is not a case for naivety. Behavior is evidence. Actions can tell us something about a person, and some people are as careless or cruel as they appear. The point is that a moment is evidence, not the whole case. The deeper cost of getting this wrong is that we become less able to hold complexity. Once we are attached to a character story, we may start selecting evidence that confirms it. A colleague who had a bad season becomes the kind of person who always does this. A family member under strain becomes impossible. Moral simplification is tempting because it is efficient. It can also make us worse at understanding people.

Accuracy over the easy story

The fix is not to eliminate a bias we may not be able to eliminate. It is to interrupt it before it hardens, by asking better questions. What else might explain this? What pressure might this person be under that I cannot see? If I had behaved this way, what situational explanation would I give myself? Am I seeing a pattern, or moralizing one moment?

Those questions make a judgment more careful. The point is not niceness. It is accuracy. We can hold people accountable and still ask what shaped their actions. Strong standards need a good understanding of causes. If we misread the cause, our intervention can become theater. We punish when the system needs redesign, or coach when a boundary is needed.

There is a human truth underneath this. Most of us have acted in ways that did not reflect who we want to be. Taken out of context, those moments can make us look smaller or colder than the fuller story. We know this about ourselves and can forget to extend the same curiosity to other people.

People may be tired, afraid, badly managed, or working inside systems that reward the wrong behavior. Someone else may judge them by character because that is easier to see than context. When we do that too often, we become less fair and less accurate. If we want to judge or work with people well, accuracy matters more than the comfort of a quick conclusion.

I do this too. I give myself reasons and other people flaws. The work is to catch the sentence before it hardens.

Notes and Sources

  • Lee Ross, “The Intuitive Psychologist and His Shortcomings,” Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 1977. The fundamental attribution error.
  • Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett, “The Actor and the Observer: Divergent Perceptions of the Causes of Behavior,” 1971. The actor-observer asymmetry.
