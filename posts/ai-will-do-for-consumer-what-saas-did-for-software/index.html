AI Will Let India Export Consumer Companies

SaaS helped Indian companies sell software around the world. AI may make it easier for India-born consumer companies to carry operating knowledge across markets.

In Brief

  • SaaS traveled because code was portable. Consumer companies stayed closer to home because trust and operating knowledge were harder to carry across markets.
  • AI may reduce the cost of sharing that knowledge, while local teams still adapt how the business works.
  • SaaS let India export code. AI may help India-born companies build for consumers around the world.

India's startup imagination was trained on one kind of export: software built in India and sold to the world.

That was not an illusion. SaaS was one of the first categories where Indian startups could build global companies without recreating the whole business in every market. Code traveled. Distribution happened online. Support could be centralized. Product quality mattered more than where the office was. In 2022, Bain described Indian SaaS as second only to the United States in scale and maturity. It also reported that around 20% of Indian SaaS revenue came from India. Companies such as Zoho and Freshworks made the possibility of global software companies built in India easier to see. The advantage was structural: a company could sell its product abroad without exporting the full operating burden.

I believe AI will create a similar opening for consumer companies built in India.

That is not because physical work disappears, logistics stop mattering, or local trust becomes easy. AI may change the cost of carrying operating knowledge across a complex business. That creates room to ask a question that once seemed harder: can a consumer company built in India also serve customers around the world?

Why consumer companies stayed local

A consumer company does not just ship a product. It has to earn trust through many parts of the experience: what it offers, how it prices, how it handles fraud, how it delivers, what it does when something goes wrong, and whether the customer gets a fair resolution. The product is not only the app. It is the whole system the customer meets after pressing the button.

That made consumer companies difficult to take across borders. Entering another market could feel like starting another company. The catalog had to be learned again. Customer language changed. Fraud patterns changed. Local paperwork changed. Much of this knowledge lived in people, meetings, and local managers, rather than in systems everyone could use. As the company expanded, it could lose coherence.

There was an Indian version of this challenge. Companies could sometimes compensate for weak systems by adding people. Labor could cost less, and skilled manual work could patch a broken process for years. In markets with higher labor costs, or fewer people available to do that work, the same approach may not hold. The underlying issue is not simply that consumer businesses involve physical things. It is that operating knowledge is hard to carry and use consistently.

AI lowers the cost of sharing that knowledge

Consumer companies handle a great deal of messy information: support calls, unusual products, payment failures, refunds, inspection results, fraud flags, pricing changes, local rules, and customers who stop converting. The challenge is to turn those signals into useful decisions quickly enough to help the people doing the work.

Traditionally, people have summarized what happened, routed it, escalated it, and explained the pattern to another team. By the time the company acted, the signal could be late or incomplete.

Some parts of this work are changing. McKinsey's work on customer care describes companies using generative AI to analyze call transcripts, summarize conversations, support agents, and identify recurring causes of failure. These examples show work that can be assisted by AI. They do not show that every part of a consumer business is now machine-readable or ready to automate.

AI can help companies compare failure patterns across markets, translate local language, flag pricing outliers, and shorten the time between an event and a decision. The business still needs people to understand the situation and act. The potential gain is better shared context, so the company can learn from an issue in one place and apply that learning elsewhere.

This also changes how we think about India's advantage. If the old path to scale was to add people until the process worked, a new path may be to build systems that help people and agents handle more of the work. That will reward judgment, curiosity, and the ability to use AI well. It does not remove the need for strong operators.

One company, adapted to each market

The opportunity is for more of the business to share its knowledge while local teams adapt the execution. Pricing, support, product quality, and learning could draw on common systems. The last mile would still need to fit each market. The hard part of operating across countries was never copying the app. It was keeping the business from becoming several disconnected companies.

AI can help a company connect what it learns in one place to the work happening elsewhere. A fraud pattern in one market could inform checks in another. A support lesson could help another team respond sooner. The company's knowledge could live in systems that people can use, rather than only in the experience of local experts.

That is the part that resembles SaaS. It is not that software and consumer businesses are the same. It is that both can benefit when a company can coordinate work across distance without rebuilding every capability from scratch.

What India can export next

Some categories could benefit more than others: mobility, commerce, consumer finance, healthcare access, and home services. They combine fragmented operations, trust, and repeated work. Those are areas where better systems for sharing context could make a difference. They may have seemed local because companies had to carry too much knowledge manually. AI may help make some of that knowledge easier to use across markets.

Indian companies also work with varied suppliers, documents, languages, and infrastructure. In the past, that variety could make operations harder to standardize. If companies build systems that can learn from those differences, some of that experience may become useful elsewhere. That is a possibility, not an automatic advantage. Complexity does not become valuable simply because it exists.

This is a thesis, not a result. We are still proving whether a consumer company can share enough operating context through AI to scale across markets without becoming several disconnected businesses. AI will not remove regulatory differences, create trust a company has not earned, fix a poor business idea, or make local execution unnecessary. Companies will still need local understanding, strong operators, category-specific ways to protect customers, and serious attention to regulation.

For years, the exciting question from India was which software company would be built here and sold everywhere. Another question now seems possible: which consumer businesses can use AI to carry what they learn from one market into another? Consumer businesses punish a company quickly when its systems fail in the real world. But if companies can keep learning across markets while adapting to local needs, an India-born consumer company may be able to serve customers globally.

SaaS let India export code. AI will let India build consumer companies for the world.

Notes and Sources

  • Bain & Company, India SaaS Report 2022. Reported Indian SaaS scale and maturity, and the share of revenue from India.
  • McKinsey, "Gen AI in customer care: early successes and challenges," 2024. Examples of generative AI used in customer-care work.
