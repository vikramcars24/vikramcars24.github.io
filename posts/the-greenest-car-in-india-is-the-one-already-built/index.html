The Greenest Car May Already Exist

Keeping existing vehicles useful can reduce the need for new manufacturing, but reuse only works when buyers can trust the vehicle and its history.

In Brief

  • A large share of a car's carbon is emitted during manufacture. Keeping existing vehicles useful longer can therefore be an important climate lever in Indian mobility, though the comparison depends on the vehicle and its use.
  • Reuse depends on trust. Used EVs show why: when battery condition is hard to compare, buyers and lenders may discount the vehicle.
  • Better inspection, battery records, portable warranties, fair tax, and financing can make reuse easier to assess.

If you care about climate in Indian mobility, you should care about reuse.

New EVs matter, and manufacturing should keep improving. But one near-term lever is making the vehicles that already exist trustworthy enough to stay useful for longer.

Architects have a saying: the greenest building is the one already built. In Indian mobility, the greenest car is often the one already built, when it can safely meet the next owner's needs. The climate comparison still depends on how long it remains useful, what replaces it, and how that replacement is made and driven.

Used carelessly, the line can sound like a slogan. I mean it as a question about how the market works. Every new car uses energy, water, minerals, transport, and manufacturing before anyone turns the key. If usable cars are retired early, distrusted without good evidence, or priced badly, some of that manufacturing is wasted. Reuse can become part of mobility infrastructure, but India still treats it as a fallback.

The climate arithmetic is the easy part

A car's carbon cost begins before it is driven. The IEA estimates that manufacturing accounts for roughly a fifth of a conventional car's lifetime emissions. For an electric car, the upfront amount is higher. The ICCT's 2025 lifecycle study estimates that producing a battery EV emits about 40% more than producing a petrol car, mostly because of the battery. In that study, the EV makes up the difference after roughly 17,000 kilometers. Over its lifetime, it emits about 73% less on Europe's electricity mix. The advantage depends on the grid. The ICCT's India analysis estimated a 19 to 34% lifetime advantage for cars sold in 2021, with that advantage rising as the grid gets cleaner.

The electricity used to charge an EV also changes its lifetime emissions. A result for Europe's grid cannot simply be applied to India. But a lifecycle comparison does not by itself prove that every existing car is a better choice than every new one. The vehicle's condition, remaining life, use, and replacement all matter.

That changes the question. Once a vehicle exists, we should ask not only whether a newer one is cleaner per kilometer. We should also ask whether the current one can keep doing useful work and avoid some new manufacturing. Peer-reviewed lifecycle research supports the idea that extending a vehicle's life can lower its life-cycle CO2 by spreading manufacturing emissions over more years and kilometers. Reuse delays new production. Repair can preserve the carbon already spent. Better diagnosis may help prevent premature scrappage. It can be a quicker lever than waiting for the whole fleet to turn over, though the actual benefit depends on what happens next.

Reuse only works when buyers can trust it

This is where the climate story can become too neat. People say buying used is greener than buying new. It may be, but buyers still need to trust the vehicle, its inspection, its paperwork, and its resale value. If they cannot, they may walk away. Reuse only delivers a climate benefit when it works in the real market.

India still scraps by age, not condition

Policy can make that harder. Delhi-NCR's age-based rules have changed in scope. In December 2025, the government reported protection from coercive action on age grounds for BS-IV and newer vehicles, while older standards were treated differently. Other pollution restrictions still apply. Age alone remains a rough measure of a vehicle's condition.

The intent is understandable. Old vehicles can be highly polluting, and a vehicle that fails a fair fitness test should go. But age is a rough proxy for condition. If a vehicle can be assessed on its actual fitness, emissions, and history, policy can make decisions with better information. Extending the life of a healthy car may avoid new manufacturing. The case is especially relevant for the petrol and diesel cars that make up nearly all of India's fleet today, as well as the EVs arriving now.

Used EVs make the trust problem visible

The point is especially clear with electric vehicles. India's used-EV challenge is often described as a demand, charging, or awareness problem. Those issues matter. There is also a basic difficulty in valuing the battery. Buyers, lenders, insurers, and manufacturers may each use different assumptions about its condition. When the battery's health is hard to assess, buyers may discount the whole vehicle. Lenders may be cautious. Useful EVs may become harder to sell, which can weaken confidence in adoption. A resale problem can become a climate problem.

Battery records are part of EV infrastructure

For years, India's EV conversation has focused heavily on charging. Charging matters. So does knowing what condition a used battery is in. NITI Aayog puts the battery at 35 to 40% of an EV's bill of materials, making it the most expensive part of the car. Yet battery state of health is not a widely accepted, portable measure that everyone can trust.

The available data can reassure, but it does not settle the question for any one car. Geotab's analysis of more than 22,700 electric vehicles across 21 models found average battery degradation of about 2.3% a year. Fast charging above 100kW averaged up to 3.0% a year, compared with about 1.5% for lower-power charging. Heat independently added about 0.4 percentage points a year. These fleet results do not guarantee any individual car's battery capacity after eight years.

A reassuring average is not the same as a comparable test for a particular battery. TÜV SÜD says there is no single accepted method for measuring battery health, and different methods can diverge by up to seven percentage points on the same battery. When the number itself is hard to compare, buyers and sellers have to negotiate around uncertainty.

The answer will not come from advertising alone. It needs standards for battery health, a portable record of each battery, clear warranty status, and a consistent way to recertify a pack. This work has started. India's Office of the Principal Scientific Adviser has issued draft guidelines for a Battery Pack Aadhaar system. It would give each pack a unique Battery Pack Aadhaar Number to track its origin, health, and second life. The EU's Digital Battery Passport is due to become mandatory for EV batteries there from 2027. What remains is implementation that helps a buyer assess a used vehicle. A buyer should not have to interpret a battery through hearsay.

Policy and financing shape whether reuse works

Policy can make reuse easier or harder. If ownership transfer is painful, resale suffers. If road tax makes it costly to move a vehicle across states, buyers have fewer options. Tax can tilt the market too, but the bases matter. GST is 5% on a new EV and 18% on a dealer's margin when an organized dealer resells a used EV. Those rates apply to different amounts, so they are not like-for-like comparisons. A private person-to-person sale outside GST is not inherently a grey-market sale. The GST Council's press release explains the used-EV treatment.

Financing adds another barrier. An ADB and NITI Aayog report reports that EV loans can have 10 to 30% lower loan-to-value and 6 to 18 months shorter tenors than petrol-car loans. Used EV terms are lower still, with battery uncertainty adding to the challenge. The buyer ends up carrying some of the cost of uncertainty.

Warranty transfer is often technically possible, but the process can be buried in brand-specific rules. It should be easy to see whether a warranty transfers and what it covers. Insurance needs ways to value the vehicle that account for battery condition. Finance needs a way to price a well-understood asset with more confidence. None of this is glamorous. It affects whether a second-hand EV feels understandable enough to buy.

Reuse also opens the door to mobility

In India, a used vehicle is often how ownership begins. In a first-time ownership market, a pre-owned car is not just a cheaper option. It can be an entry point. If used vehicles are hard to trust, people who might otherwise buy one can delay or stretch their finances. That makes it harder to expand access while making better use of existing vehicles.

A mature mobility system would help people use existing vehicles for longer when that is safe and useful. That takes enough confidence in a vehicle's condition and history for a second or third owner to make a sound decision. India is still building that confidence.

Climate work includes better information

The climate benefit of reuse depends on ordinary market decisions. A vehicle that stays useful may delay some new manufacturing. Battery information that supports a fair price may keep an EV from being discarded or stranded too soon. Policy that reduces transfer and tax friction may make it easier to find a buyer. These are possible benefits, not a guarantee that every reused vehicle has a lower footprint.

Markets can waste assets when they cannot measure them well. That is true of used cars, and especially used EVs, where an unclear battery can make buyers discount the whole vehicle. Much of the work is practical: better inspection, a consistent battery-health measure, portable warranty information, simpler transfers, insurance that reflects the asset, and financing that can assess risk more clearly. A reused car or EV needs to be clear enough for a buyer to choose without feeling they are gambling blind.

India will not decarbonize mobility only by building new vehicles. It can also make better use of vehicles already built, when their condition, use, and next owner make that the better choice.
